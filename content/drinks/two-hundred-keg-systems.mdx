Most people meet draught beer at the last six inches — the tap, the glass, the pour. I spent my years at Asahi Breweries Taiwan on the other side of that six inches: the gas lines, the couplers, the cold rooms, the hundred small things that have to be right before a beer is even allowed to reach the glass.

At one point I was responsible for **200+ keg systems** across Taiwan — checking pressure, cleaning lines, chasing down the reason a tap was pouring foam instead of beer. I trained **350+ bar and restaurant staff** on how to look after the systems in their own venues, and supported roughly **400 individual draught valves** across the island. None of that shows up in the glass directly. It shows up as the absence of a problem — a beer that tastes the way the brewer meant it to.

## Quality is mostly invisible

That's the thing nobody tells you about beer quality control: when it's working, nobody notices. A clean line is not a story. A well-calibrated CO2 mix is not a story. You only hear about the system when it fails — a skunked keg, a flat pour, a bar manager calling at 9pm because the tap won't stop foaming.

I came to like that quietness. It's close to how I think about the rest of what I do outside of work, too — the sketching, the film, the postcards. The good version of any craft is usually the one where the machinery disappears and only the result is left.

## What it left me with

Years of troubleshooting systems taught me to read a bar the way I now read a street when I'm sketching it — look for the thing that's slightly wrong before you look at the thing that's obviously right. It's a habit that turned out to be useful well beyond beer.
